The history of the printing press begins in the fifteenth century, when Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith from Mainz, combined several existing technologies into a single machine. Movable type had been used in China and Korea for centuries, but Gutenberg's metal alloy, oil-based ink, and screw press made mass production practical in Europe. Within fifty years, printing shops had spread to more than two hundred cities, and millions of books were in circulation. The consequences were enormous. Scholars could compare identical copies of texts, errors could be corrected in later editions, and ideas travelled faster than ever before. Historians often link the press to the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, and the rise of literacy among ordinary people. Yet the transition was gradual, and handwritten manuscripts remained common for many decades after the first printed Bible appeared.
